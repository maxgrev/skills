# Copy strategy

Read this reference when selecting or evaluating an audience, promise, angle, mechanism, offer, campaign, funnel step, or test. Pair it with the relevant historical calibration from [source-index.md](source-index.md): Schwartz for desire, awareness, and sophistication; Hopkins for proof and test economics; Brunson for traffic and funnel continuity; Ogilvy for durable positioning.

## Start with the market

Copy channels an existing desire; it rarely creates one from nothing. Find the strongest desire the product can credibly satisfy, then connect that desire to the product.

Describe the prospect as a person in a buying situation, not a demographic bundle:

- What happened just before they encountered the copy?
- What are they trying to gain, avoid, protect, prove, or become?
- What have they already tried?
- What do they believe about the problem and available solutions?
- What would make them distrust this offer?
- What action are they willing to take at this stage?

Choose one dominant desire. Common roots include safety, relief, comfort, pleasure, status, belonging, care for others, control, achievement, and curiosity. Do not cram every motive into one message.

Every product has a literal form and a functional meaning. A thermostat is hardware; its meaning may be waking to a warm house without wasting energy overnight. Sell the meaning while accurately explaining the hardware.

## Match the state of awareness

Use the prospect's current knowledge to choose the opening:

1. **Most aware:** They know the product and want favorable terms. Lead with the offer, improvement, deadline, availability, or reminder.
2. **Product aware:** They know the product but have not chosen it. Lead with differentiation, proof, a new use, or the objection preventing purchase.
3. **Solution aware:** They want the outcome and know solution categories. Lead with the distinctive mechanism or reason this approach works better.
4. **Problem aware:** They recognize the pain but not the solution. Name or explain the problem, its cost, and the desired outcome before introducing the mechanism.
5. **Unaware:** They do not recognize the problem or desire in the offered terms. Enter through story, identity, news, observation, or a familiar situation. Earn the product reveal.

Do not use awareness as a persona label. It is a state for a particular problem, product, and moment.

## Match market sophistication

Estimate how many similar promises and mechanisms the market has already seen:

1. **Fresh market:** State the useful claim directly.
2. **Repeated claim:** Enlarge the claim through a meaningful dimension such as speed, ease, scope, or reliability.
3. **Claim fatigue:** Introduce a distinctive mechanism that explains why the result is possible.
4. **Mechanism fatigue:** Elaborate or differentiate the mechanism with components, stages, evidence, or a proprietary process.
5. **Exhausted market:** Shift toward identification, experience, worldview, or a neglected segment while retaining a credible product reason.

Never escalate past what the evidence supports. A tired market needs a better explanation or position, not a louder unsupported promise.

## Account for traffic and context

The source changes the job of the copy:

- **Search traffic** already expresses intent. Match the query, answer comparison questions, and reduce decision risk.
- **Interruption traffic** did not ask for the message. Earn attention with a relevant hook, then supply story or explanation before the offer.
- **Referral or partner traffic** arrives with borrowed trust. Preserve the referring source's promise and explain the handoff.
- **Owned traffic** may know the brand but still varies in product awareness. Do not confuse familiarity with readiness to buy.
- **Cold traffic** needs more context, proof, and risk reduction than warm retargeting or customer traffic.

Keep message continuity across ad, landing page, checkout, onboarding, and product. A high-click ad that attracts the wrong expectation is a losing control.

## Choose the promise and mechanism

A strong central promise is:

- valuable to the chosen prospect;
- specific enough to picture or verify;
- credible in the current market;
- supported by the product and proof;
- different in a way the buyer values;
- appropriate to the requested action.

Convert features through three levels:

1. **Feature:** What the product has or does.
2. **Functional outcome:** What changes for the user.
3. **Emotional or identity payoff:** Why that change matters.

Example: automatic duplicate detection → prevents the same invoice being paid twice → the finance lead can approve the batch without fearing an embarrassing recovery call.

The mechanism is the credible bridge between product and result. It can be a process, design choice, ingredient, sequence, policy, data advantage, business model, or method. Name it only when the name clarifies or makes it memorable. A branded label without an explanation is not a mechanism.

## Assemble proof

Use the strongest proof available for the specific claim:

- live or recorded demonstration;
- independently verifiable measurement;
- controlled test or representative product data;
- customer result with context and permission;
- specific testimonial addressing a known objection;
- relevant certification or authority;
- transparent process, ingredients, construction, or methodology;
- comparison under disclosed conditions;
- guarantee that reduces risk without pretending to prove performance.

Place proof beside the claim it supports. State sample size, conditions, timeframe, and typicality when they affect interpretation. Do not turn one exceptional result into a universal promise.

## Shape the offer

An offer is more than the product. Make these terms easy to reconstruct:

- what the buyer receives;
- price and billing structure;
- bonuses or included services;
- delivery or access timing;
- eligibility and important limitations;
- guarantee or cancellation terms;
- genuine deadline, capacity, or inventory constraint;
- exact next action.

Use price anchors only when the comparison is real. Use bonuses that accelerate the desired result or remove an obstacle. Risk reversal should transfer reasonable uncertainty to the seller without inviting misunderstanding.

Offer service before pressure when appropriate: an audit, demonstration, useful guide, trial, sample, estimator, or consultation can let the product begin selling itself.

## Build one campaign idea

Choose an organizing idea that can carry the headline, body, proof, imagery, and CTA. It should reveal something useful about the product or buyer, not merely provide a clever theme.

Response copy also builds brand memory. Do not win a click by making the product appear cheaper, more extreme, or less trustworthy than it is. Let the product, customer transformation, or defensible mechanism be the hero.

## Diagnose before rewriting

When copy underperforms, inspect in this order:

1. wrong or low-intent traffic;
2. weak audience-offer fit;
3. unclear or undifferentiated promise;
4. inadequate proof or trust;
5. offer friction or poor economics;
6. message mismatch across the funnel;
7. headline, structure, and sentence execution.

A sentence rewrite cannot repair a product or distribution problem. State that plainly in critique mode.

## Test meaningfully

Form a hypothesis before producing a variant. Test material variables such as:

- promise or desire;
- awareness entrance;
- mechanism;
- offer or risk reversal;
- proof;
- audience segment;
- format or traffic source.

Judge the result by the closest reliable business outcome: qualified lead, booked call, completed trial, sale, contribution margin, repeat purchase, or lifetime value. Click-through rate alone can reward misleading curiosity.
