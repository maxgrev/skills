# Joseph Sugarman

Read this reference when a lead, story, sentence sequence, objection, buying environment, or long-form offer must keep the prospect moving toward action.

## Provenance and use

The 13 named cases match the official Wiley table of contents for the “Proving the Points—Ad Examples” section of [*The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*](https://catalogimages.wiley.com/images/db/pdf/9780470051245.toc.pdf). Several were ads by other marketers that Sugarman analyzed; they are not all Sugarman controls. Short headline and subhead transcriptions are marked **verbatim exemplars**. Case descriptions are **documented cases**. The later modern lines are **transfer examples**.

Sugarman's durable lesson is not “write long.” It is to remove every reason to stop until the smallest complete sales argument is finished.

## 13 source ad cases

1. **The Lazy Man’s Way to Riches — analyzed case**

   The famous Joe Karbo promotion uses a provocative contradiction: apparent laziness is framed as an advantage, while habitual busyness is reframed as an obstacle to wealth. Sugarman analyzes how identification, curiosity, and a large desired outcome carry a long argument.

2. **A Fluke of Nature — analyzed and edited case**

   A Royal Ruby Red grapefruit promotion turns agricultural irregularity into rarity. The “fluke” concept gives the product an origin story; sensory detail, careful selection, and limited availability turn produce into an event.

3. **Lingerie for Men — analyzed case**

   The line addresses the person expected to request the catalog, while the story supplies social permission and a plausible buying motive. The apparent mismatch between product and addressee creates the entrance.

4. **The More You Learn — negative case**

   A McDonnell Douglas DC-10 ad praised the aircraft without first resolving the crash-related concern dominating the reader's mind. The lesson is sequencing: an ignored objection can invalidate every later benefit.

5. **A More Stimulating Way — negative case**

   A Sony communication ad claimed stimulation while looking visually inert. Form and promise contradicted each other.

6. **Magic Baloney — Sugarman control**

   > Magic Baloney.

   > You’ll love the way we hated the Magic Stat thermostat until an amazing thing happened.

   The negative admission supplies candor and conflict. The story begins with the seller rejecting the name, appearance, and missing display before discovering why the thermostat works. The objection becomes the plot. Source transcription: [Brian Kurtz's Sugarman notes](https://www.briankurtz.net/titans-of-direct-response-special/).

7. **Pet Plane — Sugarman control**

   > Pet Plane.

   > This advertisement has not been paid for by Piper nor do I own any Piper stock.

   The bylined personal story, disarming disclosure, and self-deprecating humor make a high-priced aircraft feel like one person's candid recommendation rather than corporate aviation copy.

8. **Mail Order Mansion — Sugarman experiment**

   > Mail Order Mansion

   The contradiction is the hook: a six-million-dollar home is sold through the conventions of mail order. The story openly raises the implausibility, price, return privilege, and credit-card absurdity. The campaign drew attention but lost money—memorable copy is not automatically profitable copy.

9. **Hungarian Conspiracy — Sugarman control**

   > Hungarian Conspiracy

   A mock international conspiracy delays the reveal of an inexpensive computer game. Each development pays one curiosity debt while opening the next.

10. **Vision Breakthrough — Sugarman control**

    > Vision Breakthrough.

    > When I put on the pair of glasses what I saw I could not believe. Nor will you.

    Sunglasses become a new viewing experience rather than fashion eyewear. Personal discovery, education, demonstration, and a product concept carry the offer. See the [BluBlocker ad archive](https://swiped.co/person/joe-sugarman/) and [campaign analysis](https://bejakovic.com/2-advertorial-lessons-from-joe-sugarmans-blublockers/).

11. **Gold Space Chains — Sugarman control**

    > Gold Space Chains

    A topical space-age story makes jewelry feel like a technical discovery and gives a catalog departure a reason to exist.

12. **Consumers Hero — Sugarman control**

    The bargain-club promotion uses consumer-advocate positioning, exaggerated candor, humor, specificity, and a strong membership guarantee. The seller becomes the prospect's ally against ordinary retail economics.

13. **Nautilus Spelling Sale — Sugarman control**

    Deliberate misspellings become an interactive discount mechanism. Readers are rewarded for close inspection, so the promotional device makes the audience study the ad rather than merely notice it.

## 12 transfer examples

These are modern **transfer examples** from the research, not Sugarman quotations.

1. **Effortless first sentence:** “It looked like a mistake.”
2. **Second-sentence pull:** “It wasn’t.”
3. **Three-step slippery-slide opening:** “The box arrived wet. The computer inside was perfectly dry. That was our first clue.”
4. **Seed of curiosity:** “In a moment, you’ll see why we removed the product’s most popular feature.”
5. **Negative admission:** “Our Ugliest Feature May Be the Reason This Machine Lasts 10 Years”
6. **Buying environment:** “Picture 6:07 a.m. The house is warm, the coffee is ready, and you haven’t touched the thermostat.”
7. **Mental ownership:** “Drag next month’s bills onto the calendar and watch your safe-to-spend number change.”
8. **Cure rather than prevention:** “End the 4 P.M. Energy Crash” instead of “Support Healthy Afternoon Energy.”
9. **Emotion followed by logic:** “Come home to a quiet house. Its insulated motor operates at a measured 38 decibels.”
10. **Objection raised and resolved:** “Yes, It Costs More. Here’s Why You Won’t Replace It Next Year.”
11. **Satisfaction conviction:** “Use It for 60 Days. If Your Morning Routine Isn’t Faster, Send It Back on Our Label.”
12. **Specific instant gratification:** “Install It in Seven Minutes and See Your First Live Report Before the Coffee Cools.”

## 20 working lessons

1. **Accumulate broad experience.** Creative concepts often connect previously unrelated knowledge.
2. **Develop product expertise.** Handle it, test it, interview its makers, and understand its defects.
3. **Know the prospect as deeply as the product.** Vocabulary, anxiety, identity, and buying context determine resonance.
4. **Practice writing and editing.** Formulas do not replace repeated execution.
5. **Give every graphic element one immediate job.** Headline, image, caption, and layout should lead toward the opening copy.
6. **Make the first sentence easy to enter.** Clear, short, and slightly incomplete can outperform impressive.
7. **Make each sentence sell the next sentence.** Momentum precedes persuasion.
8. **Create a slippery slide.** Remove confusion, boredom, and disbelief that provide permission to stop.
9. **Plant seeds of curiosity and pay them off.** Promise a later discovery only when it is relevant and real.
10. **Build the buying environment.** Control the scene, comparisons, assumptions, and emotional frame in which the product is judged.
11. **Harmonize with the reader.** Match language, sophistication, mood, and current belief before asking for change.
12. **Sell the concept before the object.** Give the product a more valuable meaning without obscuring what it is.
13. **Research, draft freely, incubate, then edit.** Do not demand polished judgment from every sentence as it first appears.
14. **Sell emotionally and justify logically.** Desire begins action; evidence makes it defensible.
15. **Write as one knowledgeable person to another.** Avoid committee voice.
16. **Follow the prospect's question sequence.** What is it, what does it do, why care, why believe, why this one, what does it cost, and what happens next?
17. **Raise the real objection yourself.** Candor earns trust only when the concern is resolved honestly.
18. **Use only relevant psychological triggers.** Curiosity, ownership, authority, belonging, specificity, familiarity, and hope must serve the product.
19. **Edit for clarity, rhythm, and compression.** Remove anything that does not create desire, build belief, answer a question, or advance the reader.
20. **Finish the sale.** Reconstruct the offer, justify the price, reverse reasonable risk, simplify response, and ask explicitly.

## Sequence check

Before delivering long copy, ask:

- Does the headline make the opening inviting rather than attempt the whole sale?
- Can the first sentence be understood without rereading?
- Does every curiosity device lead to product-relevant information?
- Is the largest objection raised before the reader silently exits?
- Has the prospect imagined owning or using the product?
- Does the emotional meaning have a logical defense nearby?
- Does the final offer make ordering feel like the natural next event?
