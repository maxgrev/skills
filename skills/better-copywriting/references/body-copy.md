# Body copy and conversion flow

Read this reference for emails, landing pages, sales pages, product pages, advertorials, scripts, direct mail, or any copy that must sustain attention beyond a hook. Pair it with [source-sugarman.md](source-sugarman.md) for momentum and story, [source-schwartz.md](source-schwartz.md) for belief and concrete language, or [source-hopkins.md](source-hopkins.md) for reason-why proof and complete sales arguments.

## Use the smallest complete sales argument

Copy length follows the distance between the prospect's current belief and the requested action. A familiar low-risk offer may need a few lines. A new, expensive, technical, or high-risk offer may need mechanism, demonstration, proof, objections, terms, and comparison.

Do not make copy long to signal strength or short to look modern. Remove repetition that adds no belief, desire, clarity, or momentum.

## Build a natural sequence

A complete argument often contains these jobs, but the order should match awareness and channel:

- establish relevance or enter a situation;
- name the problem or desired outcome;
- intensify why it matters without melodrama;
- introduce the product and mechanism;
- demonstrate or explain the result;
- supply proof;
- address objections and limitations;
- present the offer and risk reversal;
- ask for the next action.

Do not force every asset into the same formula. A retargeting email may begin with the objection. A search landing page may begin with the solution. An unaware advertorial may delay the product reveal.

## Control the first steps

The headline, image, caption, subhead, and layout should make the opening copy inviting. The first sentence should be easy to enter. Its main job is to earn the second sentence.

Useful opening moves include:

- a concrete observation;
- a short relevant story;
- an honest admission;
- a surprising product fact;
- a problem the reader immediately recognizes;
- the continuation of the headline's thought.

Avoid generic greetings, mission statements, category history, and suspense unrelated to the buying decision.

Make each sentence create a reason to read the next: an unanswered but relevant question, a developing demonstration, a credible consequence, or a clean logical connection. Curiosity debts must be paid.

## Transfer pictures, not abstractions

Use words that let the reader see an action or result:

- “duplicate invoices turn red” beats “improved financial integrity”;
- “the latch clicks shut” beats “enhanced closure security”;
- “support receives the recording with the error already marked” beats “seamless collaboration.”

Make verbs carry the sentence. Complete one mental image before interrupting it with another. When a sentence contains several conditions or scenes, split it and link the pieces.

Use connectors early enough to signal the relationship:

- **but** for contrast;
- **because** for cause;
- **so** or **therefore** for consequence;
- **and** for continuation;
- **this**, **that**, **it**, or **they** to carry a prior thought forward.

Build each paragraph around one core assertion or image. Vary rhythm without stacking dramatic fragments. Read aloud; revise where the logic, breath, or tone catches.

## Intensify without inflating

Develop the central benefit through genuinely different dimensions:

- practical result;
- time or effort saved;
- sensory experience;
- emotional relief or pleasure;
- social or identity meaning;
- downstream consequence;
- contrast with the current method;
- verified user experience.

Do not repeat the same claim with stronger adjectives. Show additional consequences, conditions, or proof.

## Make the mechanism believable

Explain enough of the process for the result to feel earned. Use a reason-why structure:

1. what normally causes the problem;
2. what the product does differently;
3. how that difference changes the outcome;
4. what evidence supports the explanation.

If the mechanism is technical, keep the technical detail that affects the buying decision and translate the rest into observable consequences. Do not use jargon as borrowed authority.

## Create mental ownership

Let the prospect rehearse using the product:

- show the first few minutes after setup;
- let them make a choice inside the product;
- describe the before-and-after scene;
- invite them to inspect a sample, demo, calculator, or trial;
- use interface or product imagery that demonstrates the promised action.

The imagined experience must match the delivered product.

## Build proof and trust

Specificity supports credibility only when true. Prefer:

- demonstrations over declarations;
- contextual customer results over praise;
- conditions and timeframes over naked numbers;
- a relevant authority over a famous but unrelated endorsement;
- transparent limitations over evasive perfection;
- process detail over vague quality claims.

Use two-sided copy when a real limitation matters. State who should not buy when that improves fit and trust.

## Handle objections in sequence

List the reasons an informed prospect might decline: disbelief, fit, effort, switching cost, price, timing, risk, internal approval, or an alternative. Address the largest objection before minor FAQs.

Raise an objection plainly, then resolve it with mechanism, proof, terms, demonstration, or an honest tradeoff. Do not dismiss the concern or call the reader mistaken.

## Present the offer

Near the decision, make the offer scannable without turning the page into a pile of badges:

- what is included;
- total and recurring price;
- delivery or access;
- important conditions;
- guarantee or cancellation process;
- real deadline or capacity constraint;
- CTA and what happens after the click.

Use emotional relevance to create desire and logical detail to make the purchase defensible. Do not hide material terms below the CTA.

## Adapt to the medium

### Short ads

Use one hook, one idea, enough support to make the click sensible, and a CTA consistent with the destination. The ad should prequalify rather than promise everything.

### Email

Keep subject, preview, opening, and CTA on one promise. Write as a person when the sender is a person. Each email should have a primary action even when it contains useful editorial content.

### Landing and sales pages

Answer the questions created by the traffic source. Repeat the CTA when a new section has earned it. Use headings to communicate the argument to scanners, not to label generic sections such as “Benefits.”

### Product pages

Balance desire with decision facts: compatibility, dimensions, materials, use, delivery, returns, and comparison. Show the product doing the job.

### Advertorials and native content

Deliver real informational value and disclose the commercial relationship. Editorial form is not permission to hide sponsorship or delay the offer through irrelevant suspense.

## Edit in passes

1. **Strategy:** Does the copy address the right person, desire, awareness, and action?
2. **Truth:** Can every objective claim and implied comparison be supported?
3. **Structure:** Does each section answer the next buying question?
4. **Proof:** Is evidence close to the claim and strong enough for it?
5. **Language:** Replace abstractions, weak verbs, jargon, and redundant intensification.
6. **Friction:** Clarify terms, remove dead ends, and make the next step obvious.
7. **Voice:** Restore the user's cadence, vocabulary, restraint, humor, or edge where editing flattened it.
8. **Human pass:** Read [human-writing.md](human-writing.md), remove default AI patterns, read the copy aloud, and retain only the rhetorical devices that earn their place.
