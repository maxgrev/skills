# Human voice and anti-AI pass

Read this reference for every created, rewritten, or humanized copy asset after the strategy and sales argument work. Use it during critique when the user asks whether copy feels generic, over-produced, templated, or AI-written.

The goal is not casualness. The goal is copy that sounds chosen by a person who knows the product and audience. Remove model defaults without sanding off brand voice, tension, humor, conviction, or direct-response force.

## Priority order

When rules conflict, use this order:

1. truth and substantiation;
2. the user's product, offer, intent, and established voice;
3. audience comprehension and conversion function;
4. human rhythm and specificity;
5. these anti-pattern rules.

A deliberate device may stay when it clearly earns attention, desire, belief, recall, or action. A device used because generated copy defaults to it should go.

## Preserve a real voice

Before editing, note a few voice signals internally: vocabulary, cadence, bluntness, humor, warmth, skepticism, level of polish, sentence length, and tolerance for hype.

- Keep strong, specific lines even when they are less tidy than the surrounding copy.
- Preserve useful edge, honest admissions, humor, fragments, or profanity when they belong to the brand.
- Do not turn every paragraph into the same polished point-proof-payoff unit.
- Do not make all benefits sound equally important.
- If no voice sample exists, default to a knowledgeable person explaining a worthwhile decision, not a brand manifesto or motivational speaker.

## Use plain, concrete language

- Use a short familiar word when it carries the same meaning.
- Cut any word that adds no desire, belief, clarity, qualification, voice, or action.
- Prefer active voice and name the actor when the actor matters.
- Replace abstract nouns with the actual object, action, condition, or result.
- Make verbs do the work: “decide” instead of “make a decision,” “can” instead of “has the ability to.”
- Protect supplied names, dates, numbers, mechanisms, conditions, and product details. Never smooth a useful fact into generic importance.
- Say what the product does, not how the copy wants the reader to feel about it.
- Never manufacture specificity. If the user did not supply the number, result, authority, or proof, ask or use a visible placeholder.

## Words that usually expose generated copy

Remove these unless they appear in a quotation, belong to the user's established voice, or carry a precise technical meaning that no plain word replaces:

delve, foster, leverage, utilize, facilitate, empower, streamline, enhance, garner, robust, cutting-edge, paradigm, game changer, groundbreaking, tapestry, realm, beacon, landscape when abstract, interplay, multifaceted, intricate, paramount, pivotal, crucial, enduring, testament, underscore, showcase, vibrant, transformative, elevate, embark, supercharge, harness when metaphorical, ever-evolving.

Copywriting also attracts its own empty superlatives. Replace these with a fact, mechanism, demonstration, or supported comparison when they do not carry real meaning:

seamless, effortless, revolutionary, innovative, next-level, ultimate, powerful, unmatched, world-class, best-in-class, all-in-one, future-proof, unlock, unleash, reimagine, transform, skyrocket.

Cut empty openers and transitions:

- here’s the thing;
- here’s what I mean;
- let me be clear;
- the truth is;
- the reality is;
- in today’s fast-paced world;
- in the age of;
- when it comes to;
- at its core;
- it’s worth noting;
- importantly or crucially at the start of a sentence;
- let’s dive in;
- additionally when a plain continuation works.

Keep a flagged word only when removing it would change the claim, natural speech, or brand voice.

## Patterns to remove

### Generic binary contrast

Generated copy leans on “not X, but Y,” “not just X, it’s Y,” and “the question isn’t X, it’s Y.” State the stronger claim directly.

Keep contrast when the two alternatives are real buying choices and the difference is supported. “Not another app. A movement.” is generic. “Runs locally instead of sending recordings to a server” names a meaningful difference.

### Faux insight and throat clearing

Cut setups such as “what most people get wrong,” “the part everyone misses,” “here’s what nobody tells you,” and “the uncomfortable truth.” If the following claim is strong, it does not need a drumroll.

### Colon reveal

Avoid using a label or noun phrase followed by a dramatic lowercase reveal: “The secret: fewer meetings.” Write the sentence directly. Keep colons for genuine lists, labels, examples, quotations, and offer inventories.

### Forced groups of three

Do not create three benefits, adjectives, proof points, sentence fragments, or cards merely because three sounds complete. Use the natural number. A deliberate three-part mnemonic may stay when each item is distinct and the rhythm aids recall.

### False range

Use “from X to Y” only when both ends share a real scale or progression. “From onboarding to observability” is a list disguised as a journey.

### Superficial explanation

Cut trailing clauses built on “highlighting,” “underscoring,” “showcasing,” or “reflecting” when they merely announce significance. Replace them with the actual consequence.

### Importance puffery

Delete “stands as a testament,” “marks a pivotal moment,” “plays a vital role,” “solidifies its position,” and similar claims about importance. Give the evidence and let the reader judge.

### Weasel authority

Name the source behind “experts agree,” “studies show,” “industry reports suggest,” “trusted by leaders,” or “widely regarded.” If no source exists, cut the attribution or use a placeholder.

### Fake-strong verbs

Prefer “is” and “has” when they are clearer. Replace “serves as,” “stands as,” “boasts,” and empty “features” with the actual action or inventory.

### Synonym cycling

Repeat the clearest product, feature, or audience term. Do not rotate among platform, solution, system, experience, and ecosystem to avoid repetition.

### Negative listing

Avoid “Not a tool. Not a course. A complete transformation.” State what the offer is and why it matters.

### Stock marketing entrances

Cut “Imagine a world where,” “Say goodbye to X and hello to Y,” “Meet the future of X,” and “Whether you’re a beginner or a pro.” Use a real situation, problem, result, product fact, or offer.

“Introducing,” “now,” and “finally” may stay when there is genuine news or a long-awaited change.

### Robotic fragmentation

Do not stack punchy fragments to manufacture drama: “Faster. Smarter. Better.” Avoid rows of sections that each use the same short headline, one-sentence explanation, and three bullets.

A fragment may stay when it creates a clean turn, names a consequence, or matches an established voice. Read the whole passage for cadence instead of judging one sentence in isolation.

### Rhetorical self-interview

Drop “What if you could...?”, “Ready to...?”, “Think about it,” “Plot twist,” and repeated question-answer pairs when a direct statement is stronger. A question may stay when the reader will genuinely attempt to answer it or when it exposes a useful knowledge gap.

### Fake-profound ending

Do not finish with a vague aphorism, manifesto line, or metaphorical mic drop. End on the concrete takeaway, offer, proof, instruction, or next action.

### Summary recap

Do not append “In conclusion,” “Ultimately,” or a final paragraph that repeats the page. A landing page can restate the offer near the CTA, but it should help the buyer reconstruct terms rather than summarize themes.

### Chatbot residue

Remove “I hope this helps,” “Great question,” “Certainly,” “You’re absolutely right,” and “Let me know if” from customer-facing copy.

### Formatting defaults

- Do not use emoji headings unless they are part of the brand's established language.
- Avoid decorative bold inside sentences and bolding every product noun.
- Do not add headings above two sentences merely to create structure.
- Avoid bold-label lists whose label repeats the line that follows.
- Default landing-page section headings to sentence case unless brand or platform style requires title case.
- Use bullets for real inventories, steps, specifications, terms, or scannable proof. Do not turn ordinary prose into bullets by default.
- Use no em dash in short copy. In long copy, use one or two only when they beat a comma or period. Do not replace removed em dashes with decorative parentheses or spaced hyphens.

## Copywriting exceptions

Direct-response craft sometimes uses forms that editorial anti-slop rules would remove. Keep them only with a reason:

- **Repetition:** useful for mnemonic force, objection emphasis, offer clarity, or a deliberate refrain.
- **Fragments:** useful for a headline, button, caption, turn, or consequence. Avoid clusters.
- **Strong punctuation:** useful for a real announcement or urgent offer. Do not sprinkle exclamation marks through ordinary claims.
- **Promotional adjectives:** useful when the word belongs to the brand voice or a sensory experience. Pair comparative or objective praise with evidence.
- **Contrast:** useful when the alternatives are real and the difference affects the purchase.
- **Questions:** useful for diagnosis, participation, or a genuine information gap.
- **Bullets:** useful when the buyer needs to scan inclusions, terms, proof, compatibility, or steps.
- **Headline casing:** follow the brand, publication, ad unit, or user's explicit style rather than imposing sentence case universally.
- **Quoted exemplars and testimonials:** preserve exact wording and label it. Do not sanitize a source quotation to satisfy this reference.

## Final anti-AI pass

After the copy sells on strategy and evidence:

1. Highlight any sentence that could appear unchanged on a competitor's page. Rewrite it with a product fact or cut it.
2. Search for the flagged words, generic binary contrasts, faux-insight setups, colon reveals, forced triads, stacked fragments, and empty superlatives.
3. Check whether adjacent sections share the same sentence and paragraph shape. Vary only where the content calls for it.
4. Check whether the draft became more polished than the supplied voice. Restore useful bluntness, humor, warmth, restraint, or uncertainty.
5. Read it aloud. Fix places where the syntax, breath, logic, or sales tone catches.
6. End on the offer or next action, not a slogan about change.
7. Ask internally: “What still makes this read as generated?” Fix the specific evidence you find, then run [eval.md](eval.md).
