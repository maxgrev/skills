# Claude C. Hopkins — *Scientific Advertising*

Read this reference for reason-why copy, specificity, demonstrations, trials, sampling, offers, headline selection, keyed tests, and business measurement.

## Provenance and use

The Library of Congress hosts the 1923 edition of [*Scientific Advertising*](https://www.loc.gov/item/23009362/) and identifies it as public domain. This reference combines **verbatim short exemplars** from Hopkins-era campaigns with **documented cases** and test results discussed in the book and historical research.

The numerical reports are historical records, not guaranteed modern benchmarks. Old medical, nutritional, or absolute claims require current evidence and applicable review before any modern use.

## 26 campaign, headline, and test examples

1. **Quaker Puffed Grains — mechanism as spectacle**

   > Food shot from guns.

   The manufacturing action is so vivid that process itself becomes the campaign. The [Minnesota Historical Society transcription](https://mnhs.gitlab.io/archive/minnesota-communities/www.mnhs.org/school/online/communities/milestones/PUFadv1T_transcript.html) preserves a later ad.

2. **Quaker Puffed Grains — quantified mechanism**

   > 125 million explosions in each grain

   An invisible food process is magnified into a countable internal event.

3. **Quaker Puffed Grains — visual scale**

   Grains were shown at eight times their normal size. The demonstration made the effect of puffing visible before the nutritional argument.

4. **Pepsodent — problem crystallization**

   > Brush the film.

   Invisible tooth residue receives a simple name and a physical removal action. The Creative Hall of Fame records both this line and the Puffed Grains phrase in its [Hopkins profile](https://creativehalloffame.org/inductees/claude-hopkins/).

5. **Schlitz — ordinary process made proprietary in the prospect's mind**

   Hopkins described purification, cooling, filtration, bottle washing, and clean-room procedures. Competitors may have used similar processes, but Schlitz was first to explain why they mattered.

6. **Van Camp Evaporated Milk — keyed coupon scale**

   A coupon-driven campaign reportedly generated more than 1.46 million responses. The coupon sold trial and measured the advertisement at the same time.

7. **Palmolive and Puffed Grains — full-size-package coupon**

   The certificate created product trial while giving dealers a reason to stock the advertised item.

8. **Bissell Carpet Sweeper — gift reframe**

   A utilitarian cleaning device was repositioned as a desirable gift rather than introduced through attachments and construction. The buyer's occasion changed the meaning of the product.

9. **Door-to-door brush offer — service first**

   The householder selected a complimentary brush before the sales presentation. Choice, service, and reciprocity opened attention.

10. **Coffee sampling wagon — trial plus repeat path**

    A half-pound sample was delivered, then repeat purchases earned credits toward a kitchen utensil. The campaign connected trial, reorder, and continuity.

11. **Sewing-machine motor — home demonstration**

    A one-week no-obligation trial reportedly converted about nine of ten placements. The product performed the proof inside the buyer's environment.

12. **Cigar approval offer — consumption before commitment**

    Prospects could smoke ten cigars, then keep or return the box. The trial matched the actual buying question: enjoyment, not description.

13. **Reply-cost comparison — same product, different advertising**

    Three ads for a five-dollar product reportedly produced replies costing $0.85, $14.20, and $0.41. Wording and appeal changed economics by multiples.

14. **Incubator illustration — decoration tax**

    A decorative row of chickens increased space and cost by half without increasing sales. The visual failed its commercial job.

15. **Soap appeal: generic cleanliness**

    > Keep clean.

    Hopkins treats the appeal as too universal and commonplace to create much interest.

16. **Soap appeal: ingredient absence**

    > No animal fat.

    A factual product distinction fails when the buyer does not value it.

17. **Soap appeal: physical curiosity**

    > It floats.

    A distinctive observable property can outperform a more technically important but irrelevant fact.

18. **Soap appeal: desired identity and outcome**

    A beauty or complexion appeal was expected to reach a larger desire than generic cleanliness.

19. **Automobile appeal: technical feature**

    A universal-joint headline addressed the engineer's interest more than the typical buyer's dominant desire.

20. **Automobile appeal: sporty body**

    Appearance and identity were presented as potentially outperforming the technical feature by an enormous margin. The appeal, not sentence polish, was the large variable.

21. **Department-store hat — extreme item as traffic device**

    A hundred-dollar Easter hat created curiosity and prestige beyond the item's direct sales.

22. **Formula valuation — price paid as specificity**

    Instead of calling a formula valuable, the advertiser named the hundred-thousand-dollar price reportedly paid for it.

23. **Local dealer guarantee — proximity as trust**

    A nearby dealer's signed refund promise felt more accountable than a distant manufacturer's generic warranty.

24. **Pay-after-trial — stronger risk reversal**

    Shipping first and requesting payment later asked less initial trust than payment followed by a refund promise.

25. **Nutritional specificity — laboratory value plus familiar comparison**

    A vague “food value” claim became a measured calorie figure and an egg-equivalent comparison. The known object made the number interpretable.

26. **Four- or five-town test — local economics before national scale**

    Sampling, repeat purchase, acquisition cost, and payback were tested in a few markets before national expansion.

## 20 working lessons

1. **Treat every advertisement as a salesperson.** Ask whether it would help a capable seller close the same prospect.
2. **Give the ad a measurable objective.** “Awareness” alone cannot show whether the investment returned.
3. **Measure business outcomes.** Customer cost, sale value, contribution, repeat purchase, and payback matter more than raw response.
4. **Key every campaign.** Modern equivalents include tagged links, dedicated pages, offer codes, controlled audiences, and experiments.
5. **Let evidence defeat internal opinion.** Preference is not a substitute for behavior.
6. **Study the buyer before writing.** Interviews, sales calls, surveys, reviews, objections, and behavior are copy inputs.
7. **Offer service before demanding a sale.** Useful information, diagnosis, demonstration, or samples can begin the relationship.
8. **Reduce the cost of believing.** Trials and demonstrations let the prospect verify the promise.
9. **Use the headline to select readers.** It need not interest people who cannot or will not buy.
10. **Test the appeal, not merely wording.** Beauty versus prevention is strategic; adjective swaps are usually cosmetic.
11. **Prefer exact statements to adjectives.** Numbers, duration, process, ingredients, and comparisons make a claim tangible.
12. **Explain the reason why.** Show how the product produces the result.
13. **Tell the complete selling story.** Omitted proof can cost more than the space used to supply it.
14. **Make every visual earn its cost.** It should demonstrate, clarify, identify, or increase desire.
15. **Use plain, sincere language.** Literary performance and conspicuous cleverness can trigger resistance.
16. **Investigate the product deeply.** Manufacturing, ingredients, research, usage, and policy often hide the strongest claim.
17. **Treat naming, price, competition, and distribution as advertising decisions.** Copy cannot compensate for structural purchase friction.
18. **Use sampling as selling and measurement.** A sample can create trial, reveal demand, compare ads, and encourage stocking.
19. **Prove repeat purchase and payback before scaling.** Cheap initial response is not a viable business by itself.
20. **Build a positive, ownable position.** Distinctive mechanisms, names, and benefits are stronger than competitor attack.

## Test design prompts

- Are we testing a different promise or only a different adjective?
- What code, audience split, or destination can attribute the outcome?
- Which outcome is closest to revenue without becoming too sparse to learn from?
- Can a demonstration reduce more doubt than another paragraph?
- Does the first purchase produce repeat value, or only subsidized trial?
- What must be true locally before scale is economically rational?
