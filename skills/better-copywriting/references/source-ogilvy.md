# David Ogilvy and early Ogilvy & Mather

Read this reference when product facts, brand character, editorial information, visual storytelling, or a durable campaign idea should carry the sale.

## Provenance and use

The lines below are **verbatim exemplars** transcribed during the source research. “Ogilvy” here means David Ogilvy and early Ogilvy & Mather, not every campaign produced by the modern network. Attribution can be collaborative: the Rolls-Royce clock line was adapted from a motor review, and the Guinness oyster concept involved Ogilvy with copy by Peter Geer.

Useful source records include Ogilvy's [official campaign history](https://www.ogilvy.com/ideas/ogilvy-75-75-years-iconic-campaigns), the [David Ogilvy visual archive](https://swiped.co/person/david-ogilvy/), and the scans linked beside particular examples.

Study the exact product fact, information order, cadence, and restraint. Do not merely replace the brand name in a famous line.

## 25 verbatim headline exemplars

1. **Rolls-Royce — precise sensory proof**

   > At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock.

   “Quiet” becomes a scene, speed, comparison, and tiny audible object.

2. **Rolls-Royce — information gap**

   > What’s under the bonnet of a Rolls-Royce?

   The question makes engineering the story instead of announcing prestige.

3. **Rolls-Royce — reframed audience decision**

   > Should every corporation buy its president a Rolls-Royce?

   Extravagance is reframed as a corporate policy question.

4. **Rolls-Royce — objection removal**

   > The new Rolls-Royce is designed to be owner-driven. No chauffeur required.

   The copy modernizes the buyer image while resolving a social objection.

5. **Rolls-Royce — useful editorial frame**

   > A guide to Rolls-Royce comforts and graces.

   “A guide” promises organized information rather than empty praise.

6. **Mercedes-Benz — durability through time**

   > At 50,000 miles, you may begin to wonder if your Mercedes-Benz 230S will ever rattle.

   Specific mileage lets the prospect hear the absent defect. See the [campaign scans](https://swiped.co/file/under-bonnet-rolls-royce-ogilvy/).

7. **Hathaway — recurring character**

   > The man in the Hathaway shirt.

   The eyepatch supplied the unresolved story; the short line let the image work.

8. **Hathaway — provenance as quality**

   > Hathaway imports Sea Island cotton shirting.

   Origin and material replace generic quality adjectives.

9. **Hathaway — named innovation and removed friction**

   > Hathaway invents an ingenious new pin collar that dispenses with holes, clasps and aggravation.

   The product difference and three removed annoyances appear in one claim.

10. **Hathaway — attitude plus extraordinary specificity**

    > Hathaway’s Aertex Club—a damnably smart leisure shirt (with 987,693 tiny windows to keep you cool).

    The parenthetical translates construction into a memorable cooling mechanism. See the [Hathaway record](https://swiped.co/file/man-hathaway-shirt-ad-david-ogilvy/).

11. **Schweppes — human brand symbol**

    > The man from Schweppes is here.

    A recurring person gives an imported product arrival, authority, and continuity.

12. **Schweppes — visible ingredient proof**

    > You can see the lemon in Schweppes Bitter Lemon. That’s because Schweppes uses whole, fresh lemons. Juice, pulp, peel, everything.

    Observation leads to reason why, followed by a concrete three-part inventory.

13. **Schweppes — seasonal objection**

    > Will you love Schweppes in December as you did in May?

    A question surfaces the limitation instead of waiting for the reader to raise it.

14. **Schweppes — consumption occasion**

    > Coolest thing next to a pool: Gin-and-Tonic with Schweppes.

    The line installs the drink inside a familiar summer scene.

15. **Schweppes — coined mnemonic with product behavior**

    > Never swizzle Schweppes Tonic… Schweppervescence swizzles itself.

    The brand word earns its place by describing the product experience.

16. **Zippo — demonstration as story**

    > The amazing story of a Zippo that worked after being taken from the belly of a fish.

    The curiosity is inseparable from a product-proof event.

17. **Zippo — historical object as evidence**

    > Ernie Pyle scratched his shortest war story on this Zippo lighter in 1945. It still works today.

    Provenance, survival, and present performance create the case. See the [Zippo ad record](https://swiped.co/file/zippo-amazing-story-ad-by-david-ogilvy/).

18. **Puerto Rico — hard economic offer**

    > Now Puerto Rico offers 100% tax exemption to new industry.

    The prospect, benefit, and news are explicit.

19. **Puerto Rico — atmospheric story entrance**

    > Girl by a gate—in old San Juan.

    A person, object, and place begin a scene instead of listing destination adjectives.

20. **Puerto Rico — cultural repositioning**

    > Pablo Casals is coming home—to Puerto Rico.

    A human homecoming carries prestige and emotion. The original appears in [Ogilvy on Advertising](https://ogilvy.relayto.com/e/ogilvy-on-advertising-dq85bunnm5ady).

21. **Dove — overheard conversation**

    > Darling, I’m having the most extraordinary experience…

    The reader enters an intimate report already in progress.

22. **Dove — category redefinition**

    > Suddenly Dove makes soap old-fashioned!

    Dove is framed as the replacement category, not another entrant. See the [Dove campaign record](https://swiped.co/file/dove-darling-ad-by-david-ogilvy/).

23. **Guinness — useful branded information**

    > Guinness Guide to Oysters.

    The content teaches a consumption association while being independently useful. See the [Guinness record](https://swiped.co/file/guinness-guide-to-oysters-by-david-ogilvy/).

24. **Helena Rubinstein — prospect flag plus result**

    > How women over 35 can look younger!

    The correct reader can identify herself before processing the promise. See the [ad record](https://swiped.co/file/woman-over-35-helena-rubinstein-ogilvy/).

25. **Sears — price claim with business evidence**

    > Shop at Sears and save (their profit is less than 5%).

    The parenthetical turns an ordinary savings promise into a reasoned argument.

## 20 working lessons

1. **Position before writing.** Decide what the product should mean and to whom.
2. **Make one large promise.** Choose a competitive consumer benefit the product can deliver.
3. **Sell in the headline.** Put a meaningful reason to buy where scanners will see it.
4. **Prefer facts to adjectives.** Numbers, mechanisms, ingredients, and observable details create credibility.
5. **Do not bury genuine news.** New products, improvements, uses, and terms deserve prominence.
6. **Write for immediate understanding.** Wordplay must never make the offer harder to decode.
7. **Fear irrelevance, not length.** A long line can work when every word supplies information, drama, or proof.
8. **Flag the intended prospect.** Help the right reader recognize the message as hers.
9. **Research before ideating.** Product manuals, manufacturing, users, objections, and competitors contain the raw material.
10. **Build around one large, simple idea.** The campaign should be explainable without a pile of themes.
11. **Make the product the hero.** The device should focus attention on what is sold.
12. **Demonstrate the promise.** Honest proof and captions often outperform declaration.
13. **Use problem–solution honestly.** Show a recognizable problem, remedy, and credible result.
14. **Give imagery story appeal.** The image should create a relevant unanswered question.
15. **Speak to one intelligent person.** Be informed, human, and respectful.
16. **Use relevant testimony.** Fit and specificity matter more than celebrity.
17. **Build one brand personality over time.** Each ad contributes to accumulated meaning.
18. **Repeat winners.** Internal boredom is not market fatigue.
19. **Test continuously.** Test position, promise, headline, image, format, media, frequency, and offer.
20. **Judge by business effect.** Attention and awards are subordinate to persuasion, sales, fit, and durable brand value.

## Transfer questions

- What overlooked product fact can carry the promise without an adjective?
- Can the mechanism be seen, heard, counted, or demonstrated?
- What recurring character, object, setting, or information format could sustain a campaign?
- Does the creative device make the product more interesting, or merely make the ad more noticeable?
- Would the headline still help a qualified prospect if the image failed to load?
